Critical appraisal

Rubbish name - important concept!

Matthew Grainger

What is Critical Appraisal?

  • The process of assessing the trustworthiness of evidence
  • Goes beyond reading results – asks:
    • How reliable are the findings?
    • What biases might be present?
    • NOT about impact factors

Why Do We Need It?

  • Studies are rarely perfect
  • Biases can creep in at every stage
  • Critical appraisal helps us avoid being misled
  • Key in systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and evidence-based decision making

Internal vs External Validity

  • Internal validity
    • Are the results trustworthy for the participants/population studied?
    • Threatened by bias, confounding, poor measurement

Most tools only assess internal validity

  • External validity
    • Can we generalise results to other populations or settings?
    • Depends on context, population, and intervention

Critical Appraisal vs Risk of Bias

🔹 RoB

  • Looks only at systematic errors in study design, conduct, or reporting
  • Mainly about internal validity
  • Tools: ROBINS-I, RoB 2, Cochrane RoB
    👉 “How much bias is in this study?”

🔹 CA

  • Includes:
    • Risk of bias (internal validity)
    • Applicability / external validity
  • Purpose: trustworthiness + usefulness
    👉 “How useful is this study for my review/decision?”

  • Risk of Bias = one part of Critical Appraisal
  • Critical appraisal takes a bigger picture view

Assessing Risk of bias

Domain Simple Question
Selection / Confounding Were the groups comparable at the start, or could other factors explain the results?
Intervention delivery / Deviations Did participants actually get the intended treatment, and were both groups treated equally?
Missing data Was a lot of data lost (dropouts, incomplete follow-up) and could that skew the results?
Outcome measurement Were outcomes measured fairly and consistently, without knowing which group people were in?
Selective reporting Did the authors report all outcomes, or only the good-looking ones?

All tools assess the same basic things

  1. Were groups fair to start with?
  2. Was treatment applied fairly?
  3. Was data complete and measured fairly?
  4. Were results reported honestly?

Introducing ROBINS-I

Risk Of Bias In Non-randomised Studies – of Interventions

  • Designed for observational / non-randomised studies
  • Compares them to an ideal study
  • Focuses on 7 domains of bias

ROBINS-I: 7 Domains

  1. Confounding – Could other factors explain results?
  2. Selection of participants – Were subjects chosen fairly?
  3. Classification of intervention – Is it clear who got which treatment?
  4. Deviations from intended intervention – Did people stick to the plan?
  5. Missing data – Was information lost in a biased way?
  6. Measurement of outcomes – Were outcomes measured fairly?
  7. Selection of reported results – Did authors cherry-pick?

Judging Bias

  • Each domain: Low, Moderate, Serious, Critical risk of bias
  • Overall judgement usually follows the worst domain
    • It is tempting to sum domains - but this can lead to bias!
  • Helps reviewers decide:

    • How much confidence to place in results

    • How to weight studies in a synthesis

Exercise: Spot the Bias!

Scenario:
A study looks at whether people who join a voluntary exercise class lose more weight than those who don’t.

Questions:
1. Could confounding be an issue here?
2. How were participants selected?
3. How would you rate risk of bias in this study overall?

Discussion

  • Confounding: People who choose exercise classes may already be more health-conscious
  • Selection: Not random – volunteers may differ from non-volunteers
  • Likely risk of bias: Serious
  • Key lesson: Observational studies need careful appraisal before we trust their results

Exercise 2: School Feeding Program

Scenario:
- Intervention: Schools receive meals sourced from local farmers.
- Control: Schools with basic feeding program or no program.
- Outcome: Nutrition (BMI, stunting rates) after 1 year.
- Study type: Non-randomised, quasi-experimental (schools selected based on funding availability).

Identify Potential Bias

Domain Potential Bias / Question
Confounding Could other factors (wealthier districts, more motivated staff) explain better nutrition?
Selection of participants Were only schools that applied included? Were they systematically different?
Classification of intervention Did all schools get the full program as intended?
Deviations from intended intervention Did students skip meals or share them at home? Could this affect outcomes?
Missing data Were students lost to follow-up? Could missing data skew results?
Measurement of outcomes Were nutrition outcomes measured consistently and fairly?
Selection of reported results Were only favorable outcomes reported (e.g., BMI) while others were omitted (stunting)?

Critical appraisal steps

Critical appraisal

Risk of bias

  1. For each domain, judge risk of bias: Low, Moderate, Serious, Critical.

  2. Discuss which domains are most concerning and why.

  3. Consider external validity – would results apply in other countries, urban schools, or different age groups?

Key Takeaways

  • Critical appraisal = checking how much we can trust a study

  • ROBINS-I = tool for non-randomised studies
